04

Three layouts considered

A

Master–detail split
For · One list, one truth, one keyboard axis: ↑↓ to move, Enter to open, D to dispatch. Cheapest possible time to decision.
Against · A flat list buries a critical under twenty mediums unless sorting is aggressive — and aggressive sorting reorders under the operator's cursor.
B

Priority-lane board
For · Priority is spatial — a critical always appears in the same column, so awareness is a glance at one region, not a scan.
Against · Four lanes of mostly-empty columns waste the density budget, and the critical lane sits idle for hours — the operator stops looking at it. No single keyboard axis.
C

Video wall plus overlay
For · Keeps the camera imagery primary, which is what operators say they trust; the overlay lands on top of the feed the event came from.
Against · Twelve moving tiles is a permanent motion field — the one thing that destroys the salience of a new alert. Overlays also cover the evidence underneath them.
Recommendation
A, with one lane borrowed from B
Time to awareness. Master–detail alone relies on the operator watching the list. So the queue keeps one pinned region at its top — a critical band that is empty and 0px tall almost always, and expands with a banner when a critical lands. That is lane B's spatial guarantee at a tenth of its cost: one fixed place where criticals appear, no permanent dead columns. Everything else stays a single time-ordered list. Measured target: notice within 2 s of arrival without a mouse move.
Time to decision. One list means one keyboard axis and no mode: ↑↓ moves and previews, Enter acknowledges and takes the lock, D dispatches, X dismisses with a reason. The detail pane's slot layout never changes between incident types, so the four facts that drive the call sit in the same four pixels every time — the operator reads position, not labels. Rejecting C is the strongest choice here: a live tile wall costs awareness permanently in exchange for trust the snapshot already provides.
